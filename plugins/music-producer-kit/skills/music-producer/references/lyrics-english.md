# English lyric setting

Use for English-sung spans. Start from [the common mapping method](prosody.md); do not substitute Japanese mora counting or Korean block counting.

## 1. Choose pronunciation, then plan stress

Count the pronounced syllables, not letters. Mark the lexical stress of a polysyllabic word and the phrase emphasis intended in this line.
Stress placement can depend on meaning, grammatical use and variety of English. A spelling rule is not a complete pronunciation dictionary.
Word stress uses several perceptual cues, not merely loudness or a MIDI downbeat. British Council's Word stress discussion confirms the importance of syllable placement and warns that general patterns have exceptions.
Do not invent an accent or claim a dictionary was checked when it was not.

## 2. Use meaning to decide phrase prominence

Original line for comparison: `I can stay here`.
Emphasizing `I`, `can`, `stay` or `here` can foreground a different part of that statement. Do not mechanically assign every content word identical prominence.
Function words may be intentionally emphasized. Do not ban pronouns, articles or prepositions from prominent musical positions.
Music can create prominence through note length, pitch, attack, dynamics or an anticipated accent; a weak grid position is not necessarily perceptually weak.
These are setting choices to compare, not a rule that speech intonation must be copied exactly into melody.

## 3. Count weak syllables as well

A tally of stressed syllables can help a metrical sketch but does not allocate time for every pronounced syllable.
Original example: `stay with me` is three ordinary spoken syllables. `stay` can use several pitches without becoming several syllables.
Unstressed material may be compressed in performance, but do not delete it from the lyric or assignment without a real pronunciation decision.
Contractions and connected speech can change the realized form. Record the selected take rather than forcing one spelling-to-note algorithm onto all dialects.
British Council's Rhythm discussion identifies sentence stress and connected speech as interacting parts of spoken rhythm; this is not a fixed sung-timing law.

## 4. Protect the word while revising the setting

For music-first work, look for words that fit the existing accents and durations. A different synonym may help only when text revision is allowed and meaning survives.
Do not shift a word's lexical stress casually to obtain a rhyme. Compare another word, another placement or an authorized note change.
If the fixed lyric and fixed music conflict, show the specific conflict instead of rewriting either silently.
Consonant clusters do not automatically create new vowel syllables. A melisma normally continues an existing vowel rather than adding invented words.
Long notes and diphthongs need a planned vowel path and release; the actual singer or engine must validate the result.

## 5. Rhyme, breath and mixed language

Compare rhyme by the intended sound, including dialect and stress, not only matching letter endings.
Exact, near, internal or absent rhyme are choices. Do not sacrifice meaning for a rhyme score or add a mandatory unexpected rhyme.
A grammatical phrase may cross a musical boundary intentionally. Check intelligibility rather than banning enjambment.
In Korean/English or Japanese/English songs, decide whether a word is sung in English or as a local loanword, and use that reading consistently within the take.
Language switching does not authorize translating protected phrases or altering fixed melody events.

## 6. Handoff and limits

Return words and the requested assignment, with stress or pronunciation notes only where useful.
A structurally valid assignment does not establish fluent performance, native diction or successful singing synthesis.
Do not mark an unperformed listening comparison as passed.
