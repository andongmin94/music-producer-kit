# Lyric craft: expression before a checklist

Read for lyric creation or textual revision. Do not start DAW setup, pitch correction or singing synthesis for a text-only request.

## 1. Choose an intention without forcing a story

Use the supplied brief, words or musical context. When the user delegates content, make a provisional choice rather than asking five abstract questions.
An attitude, a moment, a developing story, an argument or a largely nonsemantic hook can each be the task.
Who speaks, to whom and at what moment can help establish voice; they are not mandatory answers for every chant, abstract lyric or instrumental.
A concrete object can focus imagery. A direct phrase can carry the same structural role in another song.
Do not invent objects to satisfy a quota, ban emotional nouns, or require an unresolved ending.
If the user protects a phrase or whole lyric, treat replacement as a separate proposed change.

## 2. Use section functions, not mandatory forms

In a verse-chorus song, a verse may add detail or perspective while a chorus returns to a central phrase or feeling.
A pre-chorus may create expectation; a bridge may change viewpoint or contrast. Neither section is obligatory.
A refrain inside a verse is not necessarily a separate chorus. A repeated hook can be the complete lyric for another format.
Repetition can reinforce recognition. Do not demand different words in every chorus or asymmetric line lengths.
A repeated line can change meaning through surrounding context, delivery or harmony without any textual change.

For each relevant phrase, consider number of lines, line length, accent pattern and placement of rhyme or recurring words.
These are controls to consider, not fields that all must be filled before a draft may exist.
Do not equate the source's formal moving/stopping metaphor with a universal rule that two stable sections cannot follow each other.

## 3. Make detail serve the song

Prefer a detail that changes what the listener understands over unrelated decorative imagery.
Original illustration: 'I left your cup beside the sink' establishes an object; 'I left it there again tonight' can change its implication without adding a new scene.
A direct alternative such as 'I am not ready to call' needs no cup at all. Neither route is inherently better.
Keep viewpoint and knowledge consistent unless a shift is intentional and intelligible.
Check whether a reference to 'you', a place or a time still identifies what the current draft intends.
A rhyme should not introduce a new fact or emotional conclusion solely because the word fits.
The illustrative lines here are project-written fragments, not copied song lyrics or a template to reuse in every song.

## 4. Rhyme and phrasing

Evaluate rhyme against intended pronunciation, not spelling alone. Dialect, language switching and performance can affect the result.
Exact rhyme, near rhyme, internal rhyme and non-rhyme can all serve different effects.
No target density, mandatory rhyme surprise score, banned common rhyme or compulsory unrhymed line applies globally.
Read syntactic phrases and musical phrases separately; a line break on the page is not automatically a breath or a bar boundary.
Enjambment can be expressive. Flag an unintended loss of meaning rather than mechanically forcing every sentence into one melodic phrase.
Detailed language-specific rhyme tables remain in the local library; do not apply Mandarin rhyme classes to Korean or English text.

## 5. Choose the editable side

Music-first: obtain the actual melody and phrase boundaries, then fit words while preserving locked notes and timing.
Lyrics-first: mark the intended pronunciation and emphasis, compose a musical setting, then revisit alignment after melody changes.
Jointly delegated: revise either side with a short record of the chosen tradeoff; do not maintain two conflicting section maps.
A line that is too dense can be simplified, redistributed, given a pickup or sung with a different grouping, only within the authorized scope.
When both words and melody are fixed, report an unresolved fit rather than quietly changing one.
Use [Prosody](prosody.md) for the actual word-to-note relationship.

## 6. Review against the request

Check preserved phrases, intended language, coherence, accents and the selected scope. A grammar pass is not a lyric-quality verdict.
Ask whether the hook is the intended statement, not whether every line contains an image.
When a draft is deliberately simple, exact repetition and familiar language may be correct.
Review suspected cliches as alternatives to compare, not as proof that a human or AI wrote the lyric.
Do not claim native-language review, successful singing or audio generation unless those steps happened.
