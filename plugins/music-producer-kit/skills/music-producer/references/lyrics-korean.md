# Korean lyric setting

Use only for Korean-sung spans, including Korean within a mixed Korean/English/Japanese phrase.
Start from [the common mapping method](prosody.md). Do not apply foreign lexical-tone tables or force an English stress pattern onto Korean.

## 1. Preserve wording and the intended reading

Keep the lyric spelling unchanged unless rewriting is authorized. Put pronunciation notes beside it rather than replacing the lyric with a phonetic transcription.
For ordinary full spelling, a Hangul block is a useful initial syllable unit; individual jamo are not separate sung syllables.
A final consonant is not an extra syllable. Liaison changes where a consonant is articulated, not automatically the number of syllables.
Original small example: `집에 가` can be planned as `지 / 베 / 가`, three syllables. Do not insert a fourth pitched event for the 받침.
The phrase may still use more than three melody events if a vowel is sustained or given several pitches.

## 2. Select the sung form before counting

Contractions, colloquial choices, dialect and deliberate omissions can change the reading.
`나는` and `난` are two different wordings with different counts; one is not a timing-only edit of the other.
Do not shorten protected wording merely because the source's default line length is inconvenient.
Names, acronyms, numbers and mixed-script words need an intended pronunciation. A character counter cannot decide that reading.
Keep uncertainty in the working note; do not manufacture a confident phonetic transcription.

## 3. Separate attack, vowel and release

For a long note, decide which vowel sustains, when a final consonant closes and how the following word begins.
A closed syllable can sustain the vowel before releasing its final consonant. It is not automatically unsingable on a long or high note.
Do not implement the archived recommendation as automatic replacement of ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ-final syllables or automatic shortening of the melody.
For a rapid phrase, inspect whether consonant transitions obscure the words or disrupt the intended rhythm. That is a performance question, not proof from spelling.
A singer may want a clipped consonant or a sharp ending as an effect. Preserve that choice when requested.

## 4. Keep grammar and emotional emphasis intact

Try phrase boundaries that preserve the intended relationship between words. A line break in a text editor is not necessarily a breath or musical bar line.
Particles and endings can carry meaning and emphasis; do not prohibit them on prominent notes globally.
If a word sounds crowded, consider a different placement or a smaller textual revision only on the editable side.
A more conversational alternative can be useful, but do not turn every lyric into casual speech or remove deliberate poetic language.
Repetition can be the hook. Concrete imagery is an option, not a quota.

## 5. Music-first working pass

Read the fixed phrase, identify sustained notes and rests, propose the written line, then write the intended sung units.
Assign units to events and mark any vowel spans. Recheck the meaning and the adjacent words after grouping.
When a melody is locked, changing a pickup, splitting a note or shifting an octave requires authorization.
For a guide vocal request, retain MIDI/words/alignment; do not label a guide instrument as a recorded singer.

## 6. Handoff

Return the requested lyric, optional reading and only the alignment decisions needed to perform it.
Distinguish structural fit, pronunciation hypothesis, actual listening and singer approval.
Recorded-voice pitch/timing edits belong to [vocal production](vocal-production.md), not an automatic consequence of a lyric check.
